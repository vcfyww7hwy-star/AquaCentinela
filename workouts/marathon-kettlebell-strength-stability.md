# Kettlebell Strength & Stability — Marathon Support Session

**Duration:** 45:00 · **Goal:** maximal strength + single-leg stability · **Not** cardio or conditioning

A running-specific strength session built around hip flexor strength (anterior *and* lateral), lower-fiber glute engagement, foot/ankle capacity, and the posterior chain. The intent is neuromuscular — improve force production and stiffness so running costs less per stride — not to add fatigue or breathlessness.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Frequency** | 2×/week during the base and build phases |
| **Placement** | On an easy or non-running day, 48 h clear of the long run and any quality workout. If it must share a day with a run, run first and lift ≥ 6 h later. |
| **Equipment** | Kettlebells (8, 10, 2×16, 2×20, 30 kg) · bench or sturdy box · mini-band · pull-up bar · a step or plate edge |
| **Rest** | **1 min between rounds** · **3 min between blocks** |
| **Level** | Advanced — assumes competent hinge, strict press, and single-leg control |

> **The rest periods are the program.** Do not compress them. Long rest is what keeps this a strength session; shortening it converts the stimulus into conditioning, which is explicitly not the goal here — and it measurably reduces strength gains.

---

## Warm-up / prep — 7:00

Continuous, minimal rest. This is preparation, not training load — keep everything light and precise.

| Drill | Dose | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Short-foot / arch doming** | 2 × 6 reps, 5-s holds — progress seated → standing → single-leg | Isolates the intrinsic foot muscles that support the arch. Dome the arch by drawing the ball of the foot toward the heel **without** curling the toes. |
| **2. Single-leg balance**, eyes open → eyes closed | 2 × 20 s per leg | Ankle proprioception. Hold the arch dome and a level pelvis the whole time. |
| **3. Clam + lateral band walk** *(mini-band)* | 12 clams/side + 10 steps each direction | Glute medius and upper glute max with minimal TFL contribution. Athletic quarter-squat for the walk; keep steps small and the torso still. |
| **4. Dynamic knee drive + leg swings** | 10 per leg | Primes the psoas in the deep knee-drive range. Drive the knee **above hip height**. |
| **5. Bodyweight hinge + air squat** | 8 each | Grooves the patterns for Blocks A–C. |

**Set the foot tripod now and keep it for the whole session:** big-toe knuckle, little-toe knuckle, heel — three points pressed into the floor, arch domed. Hold this on every standing lift and carry. It is free ankle and foot training that costs zero extra time.

## Block A — 10:00 · unilateral posterior chain + vertical press

**3 rounds · 1 min between rounds.** First in the session because these are the most balance- and technique-demanding lifts, and that is where fatigue raises injury risk most.

### A1 · Single-leg Romanian deadlift, contralateral load
**3 × 6–8 per leg · 16 kg → 20 kg**, held in the hand **opposite** the stance leg

The highest-value single exercise in this session — it covers four priorities at once: hamstrings and lower glute max through hip extension, glute medius and anti-pelvic-drop through the contralateral load, and foot/ankle stability through the single-leg stance.

- Long stance leg, soft knee — hinge, don't squat.
- Hips stay **square**: the contralateral load wants to rotate you open, and resisting that is the point.
- Lower until you feel the hamstring load, not until the bell touches the floor.
- Finish by pushing the hip forward and squeezing the glute — do not lean back.
- Toes gripping, arch domed, weight through the mid-foot the entire rep.

> **The 30 kg is deliberately not used here.** Past ~20 kg this becomes a grip and balance challenge rather than a posterior-chain strength stimulus. Add load only when 8 clean reps at 20 kg feel controlled.

### A2 · Strict standing military press
**3 × 5 per side (single) or 3 × 5 (double) · 16 kg → 20 kg single, or 2×16 kg double**

- Bell racked tight against the forearm, wrist neutral.
- Brace abs **and** glutes before the press — this is an anti-extension core exercise as much as a shoulder exercise. Do not let the ribs flare.
- Press slightly around the head, finish with the biceps by the ear and the shoulder packed, not shrugged.
- **Strict — no leg drive.** The moment it becomes a push press, the stability benefit is gone.

*Round: ~2:40 work + 1:00 rest × 2 = 10:00*

## Block B — 8:00 · lower glute max + soleus

**3 rounds · 1 min between rounds.** Two heavily loaded but non-competing patterns: end-range hip extension and plantarflexion.

### B1 · Kettlebell hip thrust, shoulders on bench
**3 × 8–10 · 30 kg** across the hips (or 2×20 kg stacked) · **1–2 s pause at the top**

This is the session's dedicated lower-glute-fiber exercise. Peak resistance sits at end-range hip extension — where the glute is shortened and the horizontal force vector is largest — which is exactly what a squat or hinge under-loads.

- Shoulder blades on the bench, feet flat, shins roughly vertical at the top.
- **Chin tucked, ribs down.** Finish with a posterior pelvic tilt and a hard glute squeeze.
- If you feel it in your low back, you are arching to fake the range — reduce the range, re-tuck the pelvis, and drive the hips through instead.
- The top pause is non-negotiable; the shortened-position contraction is the stimulus.
- Pad the bell with a towel or mat across the hips.

### B2 · Seated bent-knee calf raise — soleus
**3 × 12–15 · 2×20 kg** (one bell per thigh) · tempo **3 s up / 2 s hold / 3 s down**, balls of feet on a step

Bending the knee shortens the two-joint gastrocnemius and sharply reduces the force it can contribute, leaving the soleus to carry a much larger share of the work. This is the calf work that **must** be weighted — bodyweight seated raises are trivial for a trained runner, and the soleus is the muscle doing the most work at marathon pace. Expect the absolute load here to feel modest compared with a standing raise; knee-flexed plantarflexion produces substantially less total torque by design.

- Sit with knees at ~90°, bells resting on the distal thighs (towel underneath).
- Full range: heels drop below the step, then rise to full plantarflexion.
- Slow and complete beats heavy and partial. Never bounce.
- **Progression once 2×20 kg is easy:** move to single-leg seated raises with one bell.

*Round: ~2:00 work + 1:00 rest × 2 = 8:00*

---

## Block C — 9:00 · anterior hip flexor + horizontal pull

**3 rounds · 1 min between rounds.**

### C1 · Deep-range loaded hip flexion — pick one per session
**3 × 8–10** (per leg for the march)

Alternate between these two across the week — the hanging version loads the psoas harder, the march is more running-specific.

**Option 1 — Hanging knee raise** *(pull-up bar)*
- Hang from the bar, shoulders active (not fully relaxed).
- Raise the knees **above hip height**, controlled — no swing or kip.
- To load it: pinch the **8 kg** bell between the feet or ankles.
- Lower slowly; stop the set when momentum starts doing the work.

**Option 2 — Loaded standing knee-drive march** *(most running-specific)*
- **8 or 10 kg** as a suitcase in the hand opposite the driving leg — this adds anti-lateral-flexion core work for free.
- Drive the knee **above hip height**, hold a **2-s squeeze** at the top, lower slowly.
- Tall posture, no backward lean, stance foot tripod locked in.
- Optional: mini-band around the driving thigh for extra flexion resistance.

> **Keep the load light here on purpose.** The training effect comes from the deep hip-flexion angle and the top squeeze — the range that running and squatting never load — not from the weight. Heavy loading just shifts the work to the stronger, more superficial flexors.

### C2 · Gorilla row (alternating) or double bent-over row
**3 × 6–8 per side · 2×20 kg**, or **30 kg** for a one-arm row

- Hinge to a flat torso around 45°, brace hard — the static hinge is an isometric posterior-chain hold, which is half the value.
- Row to the hip / lower ribs, driving the elbow back.
- **Gorilla row:** both bells between the feet, row one while the other stays grounded. Resist trunk rotation — the anti-rotation demand is the point.
- Load is capped by your ability to keep the torso still, not by your arms.

> **This is the highest lumbar-load exercise in the session.** Of the common row variations, the bent-over row imposes the greatest compressive load on the lumbar spine. Keep the torso rigid, stop the set the moment your back starts rounding, and do not chase load here.

*Round: ~2:20 work + 1:00 rest × 2 = 9:00*

---

## Block D — Finisher · 3:00 · anti-lateral-flexion core + Achilles stiffness

**2 rounds · 1 min between rounds.** Low neural cost, placed last.

### D1 · Suitcase carry
**2 × 30–40 m (or 30–40 s) per side · 30 kg** (20 kg if it compromises posture)

The premier anti-lateral-flexion exercise, and the closest gym analogue to keeping the pelvis level in single-leg stance.

- Stand **perfectly square** — shoulders and hips level. **Do not lean away from the bell.**
- Ribs down, obliques and QL braced against the side-bend.
- Deliberate steps, active foot tripod. If your torso starts tipping, the set is over.

### D2 · Standing single-leg calf raise, straight knee
**2 × 8–12 per leg · 20 kg** suitcase in the same-side hand · ball of foot on a step

Straight knee shifts emphasis to the gastrocnemius and loads the Achilles — building the tendon stiffness that returns elastic energy stride after stride.

- Full stretch at the bottom, full plantarflexion at the top.
- Free hand on a wall **for balance only** — do not pull yourself up with it.
- Controlled descent; this is where the tendon adaptation happens.

*Round: ~1:00 work + 1:00 rest = 3:00*

## Optional add-on — Turkish get-up

**2–3 per side · 16 kg → 20 kg**

Substitute for A2 on a shoulder-stability emphasis day, or use as a pre-warm-up primer. Total-body control, overhead shoulder stability, and anti-rotation strength in one movement.

- Eyes on the bell through the roll-to-elbow and to-hand transitions.
- Punch tall through the shoulder — packed, not shrugged.
- Deliberate leg sweep to kneeling; reverse every step under control.
- Keep the load light enough that you can **pause and reverse at any stage**. Get-up capacity is limited by the weakest transition, not by top-end strength.

EMG analysis of the get-up's individual stages found the greatest shoulder-muscle demand at the **press to elbow support** and the **leg sweep**, and that the exercise loads strongly asymmetrically; the authors concluded both the up and down phases should be trained [22]. So slow down at exactly those two transitions rather than rushing them, and treat the descent as deliberately as the ascent.

## Loading & progression

Your bells jump unevenly — 10 → 16 kg is **+60%** and 20 → 30 kg is **+50%** — so you cannot simply grab the next bell. Exhaust the bridging levers first, then jump and reset reps down.

| Jump | Size | How to bridge it |
|---|---|---|
| 8 → 10 kg | +25% | Just move up. Keep both bells for accessory work: marches, hanging-raise load, warm-ups. |
| 10 → 16 kg | **+60%** | Add reps (8 → 10 → 12), then a 3–4 s eccentric, then top-position pauses, or shift bilateral → unilateral. **Then** jump to 16 kg and drop back to the low end of the rep range. |
| 16 → 20 kg | +25% | Standard progression once 16 kg is crisp for the target reps. |
| 20 → 30 kg (single) | **+50%** | Use the **2×16 kg pair (32 kg)** as the missing middle step for bilateral patterns. For unilateral work, add tempo, pauses, and range on the 20 kg first. |
| 2×16 → 2×20 kg | +25% | Clean step for all double-bell work. |

**The governing principle:** your ceiling is ~40 kg, so raise intensity through **unilateral loading, slow eccentrics (3–4 s), end-range pauses, added range, and fewer reps** — not by adding reps indefinitely. Heavy, low-rep work is what drives the running-economy benefit; drifting into high-rep sets turns this into the conditioning session you were trying to avoid.

**Load aggressively:** hip thrust, seated soleus raise, rows. Glutes and the soleus tolerate — and need — real load.
**Keep light and quality-driven:** foot doming, balance work, deep-range hip flexion. Nothing is gained by loading these heavily.

## Why this session is built this way

**Strength work makes running cheaper, not heavier.** In a controlled trial, well-trained distance runners who added half-squats — 4 sets of 4RM, three times a week for 8 weeks — improved running economy at 70% of VO₂max by 5.0% and time to exhaustion at maximal aerobic speed by 21.3%, alongside a 33.2% gain in 1RM and 26.0% in rate of force development, **with no change in VO₂max and no change in body weight** [1]. A systematic review reached the same conclusion more broadly: strength training appears to benefit running economy, time-trial performance and maximal sprint speed in middle- and long-distance runners of all abilities, with VO₂max, blood lactate parameters and body composition apparently unaffected [2]. Note what this does *and* does not say — the mechanism is neuromuscular (force production, rate of force development, tendon stiffness), not aerobic. Strength work will not raise your VO₂max, and it should not make you heavier.

**Heavy and low-rep, with full rest.** A meta-analysis of strength methods found that high-load training (above 80% 1RM) improved running economy, while submaximal loads (40–79% 1RM) and isometric training did not — though the high-load benefit was clearest at faster assessment speeds rather than uniformly across all paces [3]. Rest matters in the same direction: a systematic review concluded that rest intervals longer than 2 minutes appear necessary to maximise strength gains in already resistance-trained individuals [4], and a training study in trained men found greater 1RM increases with 3-minute versus 1-minute inter-set rest [5]. Few reps, real load, full recovery — which is why the rest periods here are treated as prescription, not padding.

**Single-leg loading is not a variation here — it is the design.** Running is an entirely single-leg activity, and the frontal-plane control it demands — keeping the pelvis level, resisting collapse toward the unsupported side — is precisely what bilateral lifts leave untrained. Unilateral work also solves the load-ceiling problem: it reaches a genuine strength stimulus with a 20 kg bell where a bilateral lift would need far more than you own.

**Hip thrusts for the lower glute fibres.** Surface-EMG comparison in 13 trained women at 10RM loads found the barbell hip thrust elicited significantly greater *lower* gluteus maximus activity than the back squat — mean 86.8% vs 45.4% MVIC, peak 216% vs 130% — plus greater upper gluteus maximus and biceps femoris activity, with no significant difference in vastus lateralis [6]. The mechanism is positional: peak resistance in a thrust occurs at end-range hip extension, where the glute is shortened, which is exactly where a squat or hinge unloads. That is why both patterns appear in this session rather than either alone. (Caveat: this is single-session acute EMG in trained women, so read it as a guide to exercise selection, not as proof of superior long-term growth.)

**Anterior and lateral hip are trained separately, and the honest case for each differs.** They are distinct qualities — loading hip flexion does not build frontal-plane pelvic control, and lateral hip work does not build knee-drive strength. Above roughly 90° of hip flexion the other flexors lose leverage and the psoas becomes the primary mover, which is why Block C prescribes hip flexion *above hip height* rather than generic leg raises. On the evidence: eight weeks of resisted hip-flexion training improved isometric hip-flexion strength by 12.2% and 40-yard dash time by 3.8% — but in physically active *untrained non-runners*, on sprint and agility outcomes, with no change in vertical jump [7]. In well-trained distance runners, 12 weeks of psoas-focused resistance training increased psoas major cross-sectional area and constant-velocity time to exhaustion, with the two changes correlated — but that study had 8 participants, no control group, and measured time to exhaustion rather than race time [8]. So: a defensible, mechanistically sensible priority, not a proven marathon-time intervention. It is prescribed light and precise for exactly that reason.

**What actually fails late in a marathon.** Kinematic analysis of 56 World Championship marathoners filmed at 29.5 km and 40 km found that **both** step length and cadence decreased between those two points, regardless of footstrike pattern, with men slowing more than women; the authors' recommendation was to train the fatigue resistance of the key lower-limb muscle–tendon units so that step length and cadence hold up in the closing stages [23]. That is the target this session aims at. One clarification, since the two are often conflated: the specific finding that stronger hip musculature raises step *frequency* more than step *length* comes from musculoskeletal simulation of **maximal sprinting speed**, not from measured marathon runners — it is modelling evidence and a reasonable mechanistic hypothesis, not a marathon result.

**Band-based glute medius work with a clean signal.** Fine-wire EMG across 11 exercises found the clam produced the highest gluteal-to-TFL activation index (115), followed by the sidestep (64) [9] — meaning you get gluteus medius and upper gluteus maximus work without the tensor fasciae latae taking over. That is why these two specifically open the warm-up.

**The RDL as the hinge of choice — with a caveat.** A systematic review of deadlift-variant EMG reports that hip-hinge variants holding the knee relatively extended, such as the Romanian deadlift, produce greater posterior-thigh activity, and that the RDL's erector spinae activation is low relative to its hamstring activation. The same review's headline finding is worth stating plainly, because it cuts against the usual framing: across deadlift variants overall, erector spinae and quadriceps are *more* activated than gluteus maximus and biceps femoris [10]. The RDL is a good hamstring-biased hinge; it is not a hamstring isolation exercise.

**The soleus is the addition you did not ask for, and the most important one.** Muscle-driven simulations of running identify the soleus as the largest single muscular contributor to both vertical support and forward propulsion, providing the greatest upward acceleration of the body's centre of mass at every speed studied from 2.0 to 5.0 m/s [11,12] — with the plantarflexors dominating vertical support until very high speeds, above which the strategy shifts toward the hip muscles [13]. (In early stance the quadriceps, not the soleus, dominate.) Bending the knee is what biases loading toward it: knee-flexed plantarflexion produces roughly 30% less torque and markedly lower gastrocnemius activation, with no compensatory rise in soleus activation [14] — so the seated raise leaves the soleus carrying a much larger share of a smaller total effort. Straight-knee raises then cover the gastrocnemius and Achilles: 14 weeks of targeted plantar-flexor loading increased tendon-aponeurosis stiffness by ~16% and reduced the energy cost of running by ~4% in the exercise group [15]. Treat that number as directional — it came from a targeted, largely isometric high-load protocol in a small sample, not from ordinary calf raises. A programme that trains glutes and hamstrings but leaves the soleus unloaded has skipped the primary engine.

**Anti-rotation and anti-lateral-flexion core instead of trunk flexion.** In gait the trunk's job is to resist rotation and side-bending, not to produce flexion. Biomechanical analysis of strongman events including both the farmer's walk and the one-handed suitcase carry highlights the frontal-plane demand the asymmetric carry places on the lateral spine muscles — the quadratus lumborum and lateral abdominal wall must stiffen the pelvis to permit single-leg stance and leg swing [16], which a symmetrical two-handed carry does not require. Among row variations, the bent-over row produces large activation symmetrically across the back but the greatest lumbar spine load of the variants tested; the inverted row is the low-spine-load option and actually elicits higher latissimus, upper-back and hip-extensor activation, with the bent-over row's advantage confined to the lower erector spinae [17]. Hence the load caution on C2. For the press, standing rather than seated — and unilateral rather than bilateral — pressing elicits greater trunk activation [18], which is the entire reason A2 is prescribed strict and standing.

**Foot and ankle work is skill work, not load work.** In recreational long-distance runners, 8 weeks of foot-core training increased intrinsic foot muscle volume and propulsive impulse, and was associated with a lower incidence of running-related injury at one-year follow-up versus a placebo programme [19,20]. Evidence that such training reduces navicular drop comes mainly from *non-running* populations and is graded weaker, with mixed trial results — so treat arch strength as the goal and any change in foot posture as unproven here. Separately, a meta-analysis of seven RCTs (3,726 participants) found proprioceptive/balance training reduced ankle sprain incidence, overall relative risk 0.65 (95% CI 0.55–0.77); the effect held both in participants with a prior sprain (RR 0.64) and in those without (RR 0.57) [21]. Note the trials were mostly in court and field sports, where sprain mechanisms differ from distance running. None of this responds to added weight — it responds to precision and frequency, which is why it lives in the warm-up and the rest periods.

**The kettlebell swing is deliberately absent.** It is a ballistic power and conditioning drill. It has its place, but it conflicts directly with the non-cardio intent of this session.

> **On the strength of this evidence.** Much of the exercise-selection literature above is acute EMG work in small samples, often in non-runners, and EMG activation is a guide to what an exercise emphasises — not proof of superior long-term adaptation. The programming layer (heavy loads, long rest, 2–3×/week) rests on stronger ground: randomised trials and meta-analyses in trained runners. Where a claim is weaker, it is flagged as such above rather than smoothed over.

## Marathon-cycle scheduling & taper

| Phase | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Base / build** | 2×/week. Progress load steadily. This is where the economy adaptation is built. |
| **Peak weeks** | Maintain 2×/week but keep total volume modest. Never the day before the long run or a quality session. |
| **3 weeks out** | Hold the load, cut the volume — drop to 2 sets per exercise. |
| **2 weeks out** | Last heavier session. After this, maintenance only. |
| **Race week** | No heavy hinging, no loaded eccentrics. Keep only the warm-up: foot doming, balance, band activation. |

**Non-negotiables:**
- Neuromuscular fatigue from heavy lifting can persist 24–72 h. Keep this session **48 h clear** of the long run and any quality workout.
- Same-day with a run: **run first**, lift ≥ 6 h later. Never lift first before a quality run.
- The single-leg RDL is the most soreness-inducing lift here. Ramp its load conservatively and back off its eccentric tempo in the last two weeks — hamstring soreness degrades running mechanics.
- Any new exercise: start one bell lighter than you think you need and add load the following week.
