Literature map for swing-lab, topic "Volume, volatility, risk, sizing and exits".
Written 2026-11-11 (system clock; the task brief is dated 2026-11-10). One researcher, one day, no market data touched.

### 0.2 Limits of this review

- Most effect sizes in the literature are monthly, cross-sectional, long-short, and include small stocks. None is a per-trade expectancy for a long-only trader entering at the next open. Every "survives costs" cell below is therefore an assessment, not a measurement.
- A Pine script sees one symbol (plus at most 40 requested series; [SECONDARY] `pine_requests.md` §3). Cross-sectional sorts ("top decile of all stocks") cannot be computed in a script. Each signal below is rewritten as a time-series version (the stock against its own history) or against SPY. That rewrite is itself untested.
- Publisher sites and SSRN blocked automated access (HTTP 403). Section 8 lists what I could not open.

## 1. Findings that change design decisions

| # | Finding | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Volatility is the one quantity here that is forecastable out of sample by simple models. HAR-RV: "remarkably good forecasting performance" (Corsi 2009); 330 ARCH-type models compared out of sample (Hansen & Lunde 2005); panel-estimated realized-volatility models give "superior out-of-sample risk forecasts" (Bollerslev et al. 2018). Nothing in this file gives comparable evidence for direction. | [PAPER] P44, P45, P48 |
| 2 | Scaling exposure by forecast volatility reliably cuts tails ("consistently reduces the likelihood of extreme returns ... across our 60+ assets"). It raises the Sharpe ratio only for equities and credit (US equities 1927–2017: 0.40 unscaled to 0.48–0.51), "negligible" for bonds, currencies, commodities. | [PAPER] P56 |
| 3 | The stronger claim (volatility-managed factor portfolios earn alpha) is contested: across 103 equity strategies, managed portfolios "do not systematically outperform" in real time (Cederburg et al. 2020); after costs only the managed **market** portfolio survives (Barroso & Detzel 2021); a conditional multifactor version does survive (DeMiguel et al. 2024). | [PAPER] P53, P54, P55 |
| 4 | A daily-bar volatility estimate that ignores the overnight gap understates risk by about the overnight share of variance. In my GBM Monte Carlo, Parkinson, Garman–Klass and Rogers–Satchell read 73–75% of true variance when 20% of variance is overnight and 55–56% when 40% is. Yang–Zhang, or Garman–Klass plus the squared overnight return, read 93–95%, with about 5–9 times the efficiency of close-to-close at n = 20. | [CALC] §3.2; estimator definitions [PAPER] P49 |
| 5 | Stop-losses: under a random walk "simple 0/1 stop-loss rules always decrease a strategy's expected return"; "in the presence of momentum, stop-loss rules can add value" (Kaminski & Lo). Trailing stops "have inferior mean returns" but "are effective at stopping losses"; costs remove the benefit of tight stops (Dai et al. 2021). On momentum **winners** a 10% stop left the mean unchanged (1.24% to 1.27% per month) and cut volatility (7.75% to 6.02%); the large gain reported for stop-loss momentum comes from the short side. | [PAPER] P65, P67, P66 |
| 6 | A stop does not bound the loss. In Han–Zhou–Zhu the 10% stop on winners still produced a worst month of −12.87% because of opens below the stop. In my simulation 11–24% of stop exits are gap-throughs when 20–40% of variance is overnight; fat-tailed gaps double the overshoot. | [PAPER] P66; [CALC] §4.3 |
| 7 | High-volume return premium (volume shock relative to the stock's own 50 days predicts the next 20 days): real in 1963–1996 NYSE data, found in 41 countries, but small for large stocks (about 0.25–0.29% per 20 days one-way) and its recent size is disputed. | [PAPER] P1, P1w, P2; [SECONDARY] R1, R2 |
| 8 | Turnover decides the sign of one-month continuation: last month's winners minus losers earn +1.37% per month (t = 4.74) in the highest-turnover decile and −1.41% (t = −7.13) in the lowest, value-weighted, 1963–2018; strongest among the largest stocks (500 largest: +0.42% per month, t = 4.91). | [PAPER] P7 |
| 9 | "Squeeze" (low realised volatility): the evidence is volatility clustering, i.e. calm follows calm. I found no peer-reviewed evidence that a volatility contraction predicts direction, and the one systematic test I found says the squeeze breakout did worse than a plain breakout. | [PAPER] P44, P56; [SECONDARY] R4 |
| 10 | Profit targets, time stops, ATR/chandelier stops, fixed-fractional sizing, OBV: no peer-reviewed evidence found for US stocks. They are conventions. They can be tested on our own trade lists; they cannot be cited. | [SECONDARY] searches listed in §8 |
| 11 | Kelly sizing is hypersensitive to the edge estimate: errors in means matter about 20:2:1 versus variances and covariances; twice Kelly gives zero growth; "the size of the wagers should be reduced". With an estimated edge, shrinking the bet improves out-of-sample performance (Baker & McHale 2013). | [PAPER] P63, P62 |
| 12 | Single-stock returns are positively skewed: most stocks have lifetime returns below one-month T-bills and the best 4% account for the net wealth created (Bessembinder 2018). Individual investors do the opposite of what this implies: the winners they sold beat the losers they kept by 3.4% over the next year (Odean 1998). | [PAPER] P73, P72 |
| 13 | Anomaly decay is the base rate to apply to everything above: 97 predictors lose 26% out of sample and 58% after publication (McLean & Pontiff 2016); 65% of 452 anomalies, "including 96% of the trading frictions category", fail the absolute t-value hurdle of 1.96 with microcaps controlled (Hou, Xue & Zhang 2020). Volume and liquidity signals sit in that category. | [PAPER] P29, P28 |

### 2.2 Reconciling three results that look contradictory

| Paper | Volume variable | Horizon | Says |
|---|---|---|---|
| V1 Gervais et al. | **Shock**: one day or week against the stock's own 50 days | 1–20 days (to 100) | Shock up, then price up |
| V2 Medhat & Schmeling | **Level over one month**, ranked across stocks, interacted with the month's return | 1 month (persists 12) | High turnover: continuation. Low: reversal |
| V3 Lee & Swaminathan | **Level over 6 months**, ranked across stocks | 3 months to 5 years | High turnover: lower returns, faster reversal |

Assessment: these are not the same variable. A spike relative to own history (V1) and a month of heavy trading alongside a strong move (V2) both mark attention and information arriving now. Persistently high turnover over half a year (V3) marks a crowded, late-stage name. A Pine implementation must keep the three windows separate (1 day against 50; 21 days against 252; 126 days against 504) and never merge them into one "relative volume" number.

### 2.3 Where this literature is contested

| Issue | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Does the high-volume premium still exist in liquid US stocks? | Positive through 2024 in one unaudited AI replication [SECONDARY] R1; decayed to zero by 2024–25 in one preprint I could not open [SECONDARY] R2. Neither is reliable enough to act on. This needs our own VAL test (H2). |
| Is the sign of the volume–reversal relation stable? | Cooper 1999 (large NYSE/AMEX, volume growth) and Conrad et al. 1994 (NASDAQ, transactions) find opposite signs [PAPER] P12, P11. Medhat & Schmeling attribute the difference to size and liquidity. |

### 3.2 Range estimators with overnight gaps (own Monte Carlo, [CALC])

Simulated geometric Brownian motion, daily variance 1, overnight share f of the variance, 390 one-minute steps in the session, window n = 20 days, 20,000 windows. "Mean/true" is the estimator's average divided by the true total daily variance. Efficiency is Var(close-to-close) / Var(estimator), both on raw values. Script: section 9.4.

| Estimator (per-day variance) | f = 0: mean/true | f = 0: efficiency | f = 0.2: mean/true | f = 0.2: efficiency | f = 0.4: mean/true | f = 0.4: efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Close-to-close | 1.00 | 1.0 | 1.00 | 1.0 | 1.00 | 1.0 |
| Parkinson (session range only) | 0.94 | 5.5 | **0.75** | – | **0.56** | – |
| Garman–Klass (session only) | 0.91 | 8.6 | **0.73** | – | **0.55** | – |
| Rogers–Satchell (session only) | 0.91 | 6.8 | **0.73** | – | **0.55** | – |
| Parkinson + squared overnight return | 0.94 | 5.5 | 0.95 | 6.4 | 0.96 | 4.6 |
| Garman–Klass + squared overnight return | 0.91 | 8.6 | 0.93 | 8.8 | 0.95 | 5.1 |
| Yang–Zhang | 0.92 | 7.9 | 0.94 | 8.2 | 0.95 | 4.8 |
| Parkinson on the true range (includes prior close) | 0.94 | 5.5 | 0.85 | 6.3 | 0.77 | 6.8 |

Efficiency is not shown for the session-only estimators when f > 0 because they estimate a different quantity.

What this shows, under GBM only:

- Session-only estimators miss the overnight variance one for one. On US stocks, where a material part of variance arrives overnight and at earnings, they understate risk. How large f is in our universe is a measurement for the research team (not looked up today).
- All range estimators read 6–9% low even without gaps, because a one-minute grid misses the true extremes. With five-minute sampling the bias is larger (Yang–Zhang 0.86 at f = 0.2). Thinly traded names will be worse.
- With n = 20, Yang–Zhang is about 8 times as efficient as close-to-close at f = 0.2 and about 5 times at f = 0.4, not the "14 times" often quoted, which is an upper bound for particular f and n [SECONDARY for the 14].
- The average true range is about 1.4–1.5 daily standard deviations (1.54 at f = 0, 1.47 at f = 0.2, 1.39 at f = 0.4). So "2 ATR" is about 3 daily sigmas and "3 ATR" about 4.4.
- A drift of 0.25 daily sigma per day changes these numbers by less than 0.02. Drift bias is second-order at swing horizons.

### 3.3 Where this literature is contested

| Issue | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Is there an idiosyncratic-volatility effect at all? | Depends on weighting, breakpoints, screens, reversal control (P34–P36). What survives is conditional: bad for overpriced stocks (P37). |
| Is betting-against-beta a real, investable factor? | Novy-Marx & Velikov: an artefact of equal weighting tiny stocks and of the beta estimator (P42). Liu, Stambaugh, Yuan: it is the volatility effect in disguise (P39). |
| Do volatility-managed portfolios work? | Yes in sample (P52); no out of sample for most factors (P53); no after costs except the market (P54); yes again in a conditional multifactor form (P55). The honest reading: robust for the market and for momentum, fragile elsewhere. |
| Does low volatility announce a big move? | Not in the evidence found. It announces more low volatility (S4, S8). |

### 4.2 Kelly under estimation error ([CALC], algebra)

Continuous approximation: growth rate g(f) = μf − ½σ²f², Kelly f* = μ/σ², g* = μ²/(2σ²).

| Bet as a multiple c of true Kelly | 0.25 | 0.33 | 0.5 | 0.75 | 1 | 1.5 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Share of maximum growth, 2c − c² | 0.44 | 0.55 | 0.75 | 0.94 | 1.00 | 0.75 | 0 |
| Volatility relative to full Kelly | 0.25 | 0.33 | 0.5 | 0.75 | 1 | 1.5 | 2 |

Overbetting by 50% costs as much growth as underbetting by 50% and carries three times the volatility. The last column agrees with MacLean, Thorp and Ziemba's statement on double Kelly [PAPER] P63.

If the edge is estimated with error (estimate = μ + e, standard error s, t = μ/s), the multiple of the **estimated** Kelly that maximises expected growth is c* = t²/(1 + t²):

| t-statistic of the estimated edge | 0.5 | 1 | 1.5 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Optimal multiple of estimated Kelly | 0.20 | 0.50 | 0.69 | 0.80 | 0.90 | 0.94 |

This counts sampling error only. Selection (the rule was the best of many) and decay (26–58%, [PAPER] P29) shrink the true edge further. Assessment: half of estimated Kelly is an upper bound for a rule that passed validation; a quarter is defensible. For a trade measured in R multiples, Kelly risk per trade is roughly E[R]/E[R²] of equity: with E[R] = 0.3 and s.d. 2R that is about 7% per trade if trades were sequential and independent. With 5–10 simultaneous, correlated positions (§4.4) the per-position figure divides by the number of effective simultaneous bets and by the shrink above. The customary 0.5–1% risk per trade is about a tenth of naive Kelly, which is where the arithmetic lands once those corrections are made.

### 4.3 Gaps and stops ([CALC], simulation on simulated prices)

Zero drift, daily sigma 1, fixed stop k sigmas below an entry at the open, 20-day hold, 78 five-minute steps per session, 200,000 paths. A stop touched inside the session fills at the stop; an open below the stop fills at the open. Overnight shocks Gaussian, or Student-t with 3 degrees of freedom scaled to the same variance.

| Overnight share | Gap distribution | Stop at | Stopped within 20 days | Share of stops that are gap-throughs | Mean overshoot beyond the stop | 95th percentile overshoot |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.2 | Gaussian | 1σ | 81% | 11% | 0.25σ | 0.69σ |
| 0.2 | Gaussian | 2σ | 64% | 12% | 0.24σ | 0.67σ |
| 0.2 | Gaussian | 3σ | 49% | 12% | 0.25σ | 0.67σ |
| 0.4 | Gaussian | 2σ | 63% | 24% | 0.34σ | 0.95σ |
| 0.4 | Gaussian | 3σ | 48% | 24% | 0.34σ | 0.95σ |
| 0.2 | t(3) | 3σ | 48% | 11% | 0.52σ | 1.88σ |
| 0.4 | t(3) | 2σ | 62% | 22% | 0.62σ | 2.16σ |
| 0.4 | t(3) | 3σ | 47% | 22% | 0.66σ | 2.41σ |

Readings, valid for the simulation only:

- A stop 3 daily sigmas away (about 2 ATR, §3.2) is hit about half the time within 20 days by noise alone. A 1-sigma stop is hit four times in five.
- The share of exits that gap through is close to the overnight share of variance times a factor near 0.6, whatever the stop distance.
- With fat-tailed gaps the average overshoot roughly doubles and the 95th percentile reaches about 1.9–2.4 sigmas. Real overnight returns around earnings are fatter than t(3) in my recollection [MEMORY], so these are lower bounds.
- Position size computed as "risk budget divided by stop distance" understates the realised loss by the overshoot. A margin of 0.3–0.7 sigma in the denominator would cover the average gap-through. To be calibrated on our data.

### 4.4 Five to ten correlated positions ([CALC])

Equal weights, equal volatilities, common pairwise correlation ρ. Portfolio s.d. relative to one position = sqrt(1/N + (1 − 1/N)ρ). Effective number of independent bets = N / (1 + (N − 1)ρ).

| N | ρ = 0.2 | ρ = 0.4 | ρ = 0.6 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.00 (1.0 bets) | 1.00 (1.0) | 1.00 (1.0) |
| 3 | 0.68 (2.1) | 0.77 (1.7) | 0.86 (1.4) |
| 5 | 0.60 (2.8) | 0.72 (1.9) | 0.82 (1.5) |
| 8 | 0.55 (3.3) | 0.69 (2.1) | 0.81 (1.5) |
| 10 | 0.53 (3.6) | 0.68 (2.2) | 0.80 (1.6) |
| 20 | 0.49 (4.2) | 0.66 (2.3) | 0.79 (1.6) |

- Going from 5 to 10 names at ρ = 0.4 lowers portfolio volatility from 0.72 to 0.68 of a single position: almost nothing. Lowering ρ from 0.6 to 0.2 at N = 8 lowers it from 0.81 to 0.55. What is held matters more than how many.
- Momentum and breakout screens select names from the same leading groups, so ρ among holdings is likely at the high end, and it rises in sell-offs [MEMORY]. Actual pairwise correlations in our signal sets are a measurement for the research team.
- Design consequence: a cap on positions per sector or a correlation-aware risk budget does more than raising N. Portfolio heat (sum of open risk) should be computed with correlation, not as N × risk per trade.

## 7. Where the literature or the practitioners are wrong or contested

| # | Point | Why it matters to us |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Most headline effect sizes come from long-short portfolios whose profit is on the short side and in small stocks (S1, S2, S3, V3, R2). | A long-only trader in liquid names should expect a fraction, sometimes none. |
| 2 | "Stops improve returns" is repeated from Han–Zhou–Zhu; in their own Table 1 the winners' mean is unchanged. | Promise the user risk reduction, not extra return. |
| 3 | Published stop-loss studies check the stop only at the open or the close, or assume a fill at the stop level (HZZ for 1962–1992). | Real resting stops trigger intraday on noise and fill worse on gaps. Our protocol's fill rule (§5) is stricter than the literature's. |
| 4 | Practitioner texts treat ATR as "volatility". Mean true range is about 1.4–1.5 daily sigmas and counts the gap only partly (§3.2). | A stop "2 ATR away" is about 3 sigmas; it is hit by noise about half the time in 20 days (§4.3). |
| 5 | Session-range estimators (Parkinson, Garman–Klass) are sold as "more accurate volatility". On stocks they are more precise estimates of the wrong quantity unless the gap is added. | Use Yang–Zhang or add the squared overnight return. |
| 6 | Squeeze lore reads low volatility as stored energy. Econometrics reads it as low volatility tomorrow. | Do not alert on squeezes as directional setups without passing H8. |
| 7 | Volatility-managed alphas were over-read after Moreira–Muir; the out-of-sample and cost papers narrowed the claim to the market and momentum. | Use market volatility to scale total exposure; do not expect it to add alpha to every signal. |
| 8 | Volume "confirmation" rules treat all high volume alike. The literature separates a shock against own history, a month of high turnover, and chronically high turnover, with different signs at different horizons (§2.2). | Three separate features. |
| 9 | Kelly is quoted as a sizing recommendation. The papers that champion it say the inputs are too uncertain to bet it in full (P63). | Kelly is a ceiling; shrink by t²/(1+t²) and again for decay. |
| 10 | Bessembinder is cited for "pick the big winners". The paper's own conclusion is about why concentrated portfolios underperform. | Supports letting winners run and argues against a 5-name book at the same time. |
| 11 | Replication: Hou–Xue–Zhang (most anomalies fail) versus Jensen–Kelly–Pedersen (most replicate). They differ in method and in what counts as failure. | Either way the surviving magnitudes are smaller than first published; apply the McLean–Pontiff haircut to priors. |

## Hypotheses to test

Common frame (protocol §§2–5): universe = median 60-day dollar volume ≥ $20M and price ≥ $5 at the signal date; signal at the close of daily bar t using data up to t; entry at the open of t+1; costs 5/10/25 bp per side by liquidity bucket; inference on the daily series of signal-minus-control differences with Newey–West errors; DEV for exploration, VAL for confirmation. "σ" is the forecast daily sigma from H1's winning estimator. Priors are my probability that the stated effect survives VAL and then out-of-sample testing net of costs, after the McLean–Pontiff haircut.
